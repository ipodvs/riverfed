# Index fund basics — why passive beats most active investors

A short primer on low-cost index investing.

## The core argument

An index fund buys every stock in a market index (like the S&P 500) in
proportion to its size, instead of paying a manager to pick winners. Over
20-year periods, roughly 90% of actively managed US equity funds
underperform their benchmark index after fees (SPIVA scorecards, S&P
Global). The math is structural, not cyclical: the average actively
managed dollar earns the market return *minus* costs, so the average
active fund must lag the index by its expense ratio.

## Costs compound against you

A 1% annual fee sounds small. On a 7% nominal return over 30 years it
consumes roughly a quarter of the final balance. Vanguard's S&P 500 ETF
(VOO) charges 0.03% — thirty times cheaper than the typical active
mutual fund. John Bogle called this "the relentless rules of humble
arithmetic": gross return minus costs equals net return, and costs are
the only lever an investor fully controls.

## Dollar-cost averaging

Investing a fixed amount on a fixed schedule (say, monthly) removes the
temptation to time the market. You buy more shares when prices are low
and fewer when they are high. Studies show lump-sum investing beats DCA
about two-thirds of the time (because markets trend up), but DCA wins on
behavior: people actually stick with it through drawdowns.

## The three-fund portfolio

A classic lazy portfolio popularized by the Bogleheads community:

1. Total US stock market index fund
2. Total international stock index fund
3. Total bond market index fund

Rebalance once a year. Asset allocation (the stock/bond split) drives
the vast majority of portfolio variance — far more than fund selection
or market timing.

## Key terms

- **Expense ratio** — the annual fee a fund charges, as a % of assets.
- **ETF** — exchange-traded fund; an index fund you can trade like a stock.
- **Rebalancing** — selling what grew past its target weight and buying
  what shrank, to restore the intended risk level.
